# Partner Growth Method

A seven-stage operating system for building, measuring, and scaling B2B partner programs.

---

## The problem

Most partner programs stall at basic. They recruit partners, sign contracts, and then wait for revenue that arrives slowly, inconsistently, or not at all. The pattern is predictable: a company builds a partner portal, hires a channel manager, funds some MDF, and declares the program launched. Twelve months later the CEO asks why partner-sourced pipeline sits at single-digit percentages of total revenue.

The root cause is structural, not motivational. Companies treat partnerships as a support function bolted onto direct sales. They inherit a channel management mindset from the 1990s distribution era and apply it to an ecosystem economy that operates on different physics. [P-01] [P-03]

Three specific failures recur:

First, partner programs lack a lifecycle. Companies invest heavily in recruitment and ignore everything between signed contract and first revenue. The gap between signing a partner and that partner generating consistent pipeline is where most programs die.

Second, there is no diagnostic. Companies cannot answer the question "how mature is our partner program?" with anything more precise than a feeling. Without measurement, there is no roadmap. Without a roadmap, investment decisions are guesswork.

Third, partner programs that cannot prove ROI lose executive sponsorship and budget. A Forrester study of 454 companies found that high-maturity programs drive company-level revenue growth at nearly 2x the rate of low-maturity programs: 30% versus 16-17%. Those same high-maturity companies report 28% of total revenue from partnerships, compared to 18% for programs still operating at basic. The gap between high and low maturity is not marginal. It is $162M in average annual partner-channel revenue for a $1.5B company. Yet only 6% of partnership teams report operating at full capacity, and over 50% report insufficient budget for growth. Programs that cannot produce finance-ready reporting on a monthly cadence get defunded, regardless of their actual contribution. [P-04] [P-06]

The cost of inaction compounds. A competitor with a mature ecosystem captures partner mindshare, locks in co-sell relationships, and builds the install base data that feeds renewals and expansion. By the time a CEO notices the gap, the competitor's partners have already embedded themselves in the customer's buying process. The operating system that converts partner relationships into revenue is the differentiator. Access to partners is not.

## The seven-stage lifecycle

Partner programs are not projects with a beginning and an end. They are operating systems with seven stages that run continuously. Each stage has a specific job, specific failure modes, and specific metrics. Skipping a stage or running one poorly creates debt that compounds downstream. [P-09]

**Stage 1: Recruit**

Identify, qualify, and sign the right partners. Not the most partners. The right partners. This stage applies Target Account Selection discipline to the partner search: ideal partner profile, scoring matrix, prioritization grid. The output is a curated portfolio of partners whose capabilities, market access, and commercial incentives align with the company's growth plan. Most programs fail here by optimizing for quantity over fit. [P-12] [P-13]

See: [01-recruit.md](01-recruit.md)

**Stage 2: Onboard**

Move a signed partner from contract to first transaction. Onboarding is not orientation. It is the structured transfer of product knowledge, commercial terms, operational process, and system access that a partner needs to close their first deal without hand-holding. The metric is time-to-first-revenue, not time-to-portal-login. Programs that treat onboarding as a webinar and a PDF lose partners in the first 90 days.

See: [02-onboard.md](02-onboard.md)

**Stage 3: Enable**

Build the partner's capacity to sell and deliver independently. Enablement covers training, content, tools, and certification. The goal is partner autonomy: a partner who can qualify opportunities, configure solutions, handle objections, and close deals without calling the manufacturer for every question. This stage separates programs that scale from programs that stay founder-dependent.

See: [03-enable.md](03-enable.md)

**Stage 4: Co-sell**

Generate joint pipeline and pursue deals together. Co-selling is not referral passing. It is structured collaboration: shared account mapping, joint value propositions, coordinated campaigns, and integrated pipeline management. The Account Mapping Matrix is the base artifact. Partners who co-sell outperform partners who resell by a factor the data makes undeniable. [P-05] [P-11]

See: [04-cosell.md](04-cosell.md)

**Stage 5: Deliver**

Execute from signed order through commissioned-and-handed-over installation. The partner owns commercial responsibility for end-customer satisfaction. The manufacturer provides product, technical sign-off, and back-office discipline. Deliver is where the partnership either compounds trust or accumulates debt. Every handover must produce install base data: serial numbers, end-customer contacts, warranty terms. Without this, Stages 6 and 7 have no foundation. [P-16] [P-21]

See: [05-deliver.md](05-deliver.md)

**Stage 6: Renew**

Monetize the installed base through seven service revenue streams: maintenance contracts, ad-hoc call-outs, spare parts, retrofit, modernization, remote monitoring, and digital services. For industrial B2B manufacturers, service revenue represents 30%+ of total income at top performers, with gross margins 2x equipment margins and EBIT margins 3-7x higher. The install base data captured in Stage 5 is the foundation: every registered unit becomes a renewable, serviceable, upgradeable asset. Companies that treat service as a cost center lose the highest-margin revenue stream in their business to third-party providers and competitors. At World-class maturity, connected equipment enables predictive maintenance and performance-based contracts where the service fee is tied to measurable uptime outcomes. [P-44]

See: [06-renew.md](06-renew.md)

**Stage 7: Expand**

Cross-sell, upsell, and open new segments or geographies through the partner portfolio. Expansion is the compound interest of a mature partner program. It requires portfolio-level thinking: which partners serve which segments, where do coverage gaps exist, and which adjacent markets can the existing partner base reach without new recruitment. Constellation management, not individual partner management, drives this stage. [P-14]

See: [07-expand.md](07-expand.md)

## The maturity model

Knowing the seven stages is not enough. A CEO needs to know where the company stands today, what "good" looks like, and what it takes to get there. The maturity model answers all three.

Every stage is scored at one of three levels:

**Basic.** The stage exists but runs on ad hoc effort. Processes are manual, results are inconsistent, and the work depends on individual heroism rather than institutional capability. Most partner programs operate here across most stages. [P-40]

**Professional.** The stage runs on documented process with clear ownership. Metrics are tracked. SLAs exist and are enforced. Outcomes are predictable within reasonable variance. Getting from basic to professional is primarily a systems and governance investment.

**World-class.** The stage runs with minimal friction, high automation, and continuous optimization. Partner-side autonomy is high. Data flows both directions. The company sets the standard that competitors benchmark against. Getting from professional to world-class requires both technology investment and a cultural shift toward ecosystem thinking.

The 7x3 matrix:

| Stage | Basic | Professional | World-class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recruit | Ad hoc sourcing | Systematic with ideal partner profile | Ecosystem-led with data-driven matching |
| Onboard | Manual, inconsistent | Standardized with SLAs | Automated and personalized per partner type |
| Enable | Reactive, request-driven | Programmatic with certification | Self-service platform with continuous refresh |
| Co-sell | Opportunistic | Structured with joint planning | Data-driven with co-investment model |
| Deliver | Inconsistent execution | Quality-assured with gates | Partner-autonomous with real-time visibility |
| Renew | Ad hoc service, basic contracts | Seven streams priced, fleet 30%+ connected | Predictive maintenance, performance-based contracts |
| Expand | None or sporadic | Account-planned per partner | Portfolio-orchestrated across segments |

Each cell contains 3-5 capability checkpoints and 2-3 KPIs with quantified thresholds. The full scorecard is a working diagnostic tool, not a conceptual framework. [P-36]

Note: Basic/Professional/World-class are maturity levels that score the company's partner program. They are separate from partner tiers (Silver/Gold/Platinum or equivalent) that classify individual partners within the program. A company at Professional maturity may have Silver, Gold, and Platinum partners. The maturity model measures the program. The tier model measures the partner.

## The diagnostic process

The maturity model becomes actionable through a structured diagnostic. The process takes a company from self-assessment to scored roadmap in four steps:

**Step 1: intake.** A 10-question strategic questionnaire establishes the company's current state: value proposition, partner mix, revenue share, pain points, and 12-month ambition. Optional deep modules cover PESTEL, competitive landscape, value chain, and technology stack.

**Step 2: scoring.** Each of the seven stages receives a maturity level based on capability evidence, not self-reported perception. The scoring applies the checkpoints from the maturity model. A company either has an ideal partner profile with a scoring matrix (Professional in Recruit) or it does not (Basic). Binary clarity eliminates ambiguity. [P-36]

**Step 3: gap map.** The diagnostic produces a visual: current scores plotted against a target state. The spider chart makes the gap visible in one second. Seven axes, three concentric rings. Any CEO can read it without explanation. The delta between current and target is the roadmap.

**Step 4: roadmap.** The gap map converts to a sequenced action plan. Stages are not all equally urgent. A company with strong recruitment but weak enablement has a different 90-day priority than one with strong enablement but no co-sell motion. The intervention library in each stage document provides the specific plays. The roadmap selects the 3-5 plays with the highest ROI for the company's specific gap profile.

## Proof

The economics of partner program maturity are documented by third-party research, not vendor marketing.

Forrester surveyed 454 companies across the US, Europe, and APAC and found that high-maturity partnership programs drive 2x faster company-level revenue growth (30% vs 16-17%), generate 2.3x faster partnership channel growth (28% vs 12%), and are 5x more likely to exceed stakeholder expectations on stock price and bottom-line profitability. High-maturity companies receive 28% of total revenue from partnerships compared to 18% for low-maturity programs, a difference of $162M annually for the average company in the sample. McKinsey's analysis of 100 incumbent ecosystem initiatives confirmed the difficulty: while 55% gained customer traction, only 10-15% generated more than 5% of total revenue from ecosystem value propositions. The difference between the 55% and the 10-15% is execution maturity, not strategic intent.

In partner co-selling specifically: companies that frequently co-sell report 51% higher revenue growth than those that do not. 68% of companies report higher close rates when partners are involved. Partners who complete certification programs generate 6x more revenue than uncertified partners. These are not marginal effects. They are structural advantages that compound over time.

This method was built from operational experience running pan-European partner programs across 15+ countries with mixed partner types, and stress-tested against an eight-book evidence base spanning ecosystem strategy, alliance governance, channel marketing, and partnership economics. The source corpus includes ecosystem-led growth (Moore), the partnership economy (Yovanno), ecosystem strategy at the sector level (Atluri/Dietz, McKinsey), alliance governance (Bamford/Gomes-Casseres), and channel marketing operations (Moore/Thomas). The 50 principles in the evidence library are synthesized from these sources, mapped to the seven lifecycle stages, and validated against operational deployment.

The method has been applied in a live transformation at a European B2B manufacturer operating across 15+ countries with a mixed partner base of resellers, system integrators, local service providers, and distributors. The Deliver stage was the first to be rebuilt from the ground up: ad hoc order processing replaced with a gated, tier-differentiated system scored on 9 KPIs, with RACI mapped across manufacturer, partner, and end-customer. Application-specific results and proof metrics are documented in the HVO layer, not in the method itself, because the method is portable and the proof must be replicable per target company.

The method's portability is the point. The diagnostic applies to any B2B company selling through partners, regardless of industry, product complexity, or geographic footprint. The stage content and maturity thresholds are universal. The intervention library specializes per company through the intake and diagnostic process.

## Tool landscape

Technology either accelerates or bottlenecks the partner lifecycle. There is no neutral position.

Only 14.6% of companies report having a fully integrated, purpose-built partner technology stack. The majority run partnerships on spreadsheets, CRM workarounds, and disconnected point solutions. 42.5% of partnership leaders cite tools and systems gaps as a primary constraint on growth. The single most commonly reported challenge is limited visibility into partner performance (26.8%), which traces directly to disconnected data across onboarding, deal registration, enablement, and financial systems. A company cannot score its own maturity, run a diagnostic, or produce the finance-ready reporting that secures executive sponsorship if the data required for those activities lives in five systems that do not talk to each other.

The method requires technology across eight categories. It is vendor-neutral. It defines what each category must do for the partner lifecycle, not which product to buy.

**PRM (Partner Relationship Management).** The system of record for partner profiles, tiers, deal registration, and portal experience. The coordination layer across all seven stages. Best evaluated as a governance framework embedded within CRM, not as a standalone portal.

**Ecosystem operations and data mapping.** The layer that connects partner data to the company's CRM and maps overlapping customer bases without raw list exchange. The Account Mapping Matrix lives here. Critical for Co-sell and Expand. [P-42] [P-43]

**CRM.** Extended to carry partner attribution on opportunities and closed deals. Without this, partner-sourced and partner-influenced revenue remain invisible. Attribution integrity is the single largest trust factor in partner co-selling: partners who believe the vendor will reclassify their sourced deals as direct stop sourcing.

**Marketing automation.** Extended to support the four partner marketing motions: to partners, with partners, through partners, and for partners. Each motion maps to specific lifecycle stages. [P-31] [P-32] [P-33] [P-34]

**Learning management.** Certification tracking, training content delivery, and competency verification for Stage 3 (Enable). Partners who complete certification generate 6x more revenue. The LMS must integrate with CRM to correlate learning data with sales outcomes.

**Deal registration and CPQ.** Protection of partner-sourced deals and accurate quoting for configured products. Relevant to Co-sell and Deliver. Speed of approval workflow directly affects partner willingness to register deals.

**Analytics and BI.** The measurement layer that feeds the diagnostic. Must pull data from PRM, CRM, and financial systems to produce the maturity scorecard and spider chart. Without this integration, the diagnostic runs on estimates instead of evidence.

**Collaboration.** Joint planning, shared documents, and communication channels between manufacturer and partner teams. The operational substrate of every stage.

The critical requirement is integration, not any single product. The State of Partner Operations research found integration capability to be the single most important factor in partner technology purchase decisions. Each stage document specifies tool requirements with evaluation criteria. The full category map and evaluation framework are maintained separately.
