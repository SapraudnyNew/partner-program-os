# Stage 1: Recruit

## Strategic intent

Find and sign the right partners. Not the most partners. The right partners.

Recruit is the intake valve of the partner lifecycle. Every decision made here compounds through the remaining six stages. A partner recruited for the wrong reasons consumes onboarding resources, absorbs enablement budget, generates no co-sell pipeline, delivers poorly, churns before renewal, and never expands. The cost is not the recruitment effort. The cost is the six stages of wasted capacity downstream.

Most partner programs fail at Recruit by optimizing for quantity. They announce an open partner program, accept all applicants, and end up with a portfolio where 20% of partners generate 80% of revenue while the remaining 80% consume support resources and produce nothing. The method inverts this: define what a high-performing partner looks like first, then recruit selectively against that profile. [P-09] [P-12]

## The system

### Partner type taxonomy

Before recruiting, classify the partner types the business needs. Each type serves a different commercial purpose, requires different contractual terms, and produces different economics. Treating all partners identically is the most common structural error in recruitment. [P-13]

| Partner type | Commercial purpose | Revenue model | Typical contract |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reseller | Buy and resell product to end-customer | Margin on resale (15-40% depending on tier and volume) | Distribution agreement with territory and exclusivity terms |
| System Integrator (SI) | Design, install, and integrate product into larger solution | Project fee + product margin | Framework agreement with project-by-project SOWs |
| Local Service Provider (LSP) | Install, maintain, and service product post-sale | Service fee + parts margin + service contract revenue | Service partner agreement with certification requirements |
| Distributor | Aggregate demand, hold inventory, provide logistics and credit | Distribution margin (5-15%) on throughput | Master distribution agreement with inventory and payment terms |
| Technology partner | Integrate product with complementary technology | Revenue share, referral fee, or joint solution pricing | Technology partnership agreement with integration SLAs |
| Referral partner | Identify and introduce opportunities without transacting | Referral fee (fixed or % of first-year revenue) | Referral agreement with attribution rules and payment triggers |

A company does not need all six types. The Build/Acquire/Partner grid (below) determines which capabilities to source through partners and which types serve those capabilities. [P-10]

### Build/Acquire/Partner grid

Before recruiting, answer one question per capability the business needs: build it internally, acquire it through M&A, or source it through a partner? This grid prevents the common mistake of recruiting partners for capabilities the company should build, or building capabilities a partner already has. [P-10]

| Capability needed | Build | Acquire | Partner | Decision criteria |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Capability 1] | | | | Speed to market, cost, control, strategic importance |
| [Capability 2] | | | | Same criteria |
| [Capability N] | | | | Same criteria |

Decision criteria per cell:

- Build when: capability is core to competitive advantage, speed is secondary, long-term control matters.
- Acquire when: capability is available in the market, speed matters, integration risk is manageable.
- Partner when: capability is non-core but essential for customer delivery, partner has superior market access, or the company cannot justify the investment to build or acquire.

Only capabilities marked "Partner" enter the recruitment pipeline. This focuses recruitment effort on the gaps that partners actually fill.

### ABM/TAS skill: Ideal Partner Profile

The Ideal Partner Profile (IPP) is to partner recruitment what the Ideal Customer Profile is to direct sales. It defines what a high-value partner looks like across five dimensions, each weighted by strategic importance. [P-12]

**The five dimensions:**

**1. Strategic fit (weight: 25-35%)**

Does the partner's business direction align with the company's growth plan? A partner expanding into the same geographies, verticals, or customer segments the company targets has natural strategic fit. A partner retreating from those segments is a misfit regardless of current revenue.

Criteria to score:
- Geographic overlap with target markets
- Vertical specialization aligned with company focus
- Growth trajectory (expanding, stable, contracting)
- Strategic priority assigned to the company's product category
- Willingness to invest in the relationship (not just purchase volume) [P-12]

**2. Capability (weight: 20-30%)**

Can the partner sell, deliver, and service the product? Capability is not aspiration. It is current, demonstrable skill.

Criteria to score:
- Technical certifications held (relevant to the product)
- Installed base of similar or complementary products
- Pre-sales engineering capacity
- Post-sales service and maintenance capacity
- Project management maturity (for complex installations)

**3. Market access (weight: 20-25%)**

Does the partner reach customers the company cannot reach efficiently through direct sales? Market access is the primary economic justification for partnering.

Criteria to score:
- Customer base size and overlap with company target accounts
- Relationships with decision-makers at target accounts
- Sales team size and coverage in target geographies
- Existing contracts or frameworks with target accounts
- Brand recognition in the target segment

**4. Financial health (weight: 10-15%)**

Can the partner sustain the investment required to sell and deliver? Partners under financial stress take on deals they cannot execute, delay payments, and create downstream risk in Deliver and Renew.

Criteria to score:
- Revenue size and trend (minimum threshold depends on product complexity)
- Profitability and cash position
- Credit rating or payment history
- Dependency on any single vendor (concentration risk)
- Investment capacity for training, demo equipment, and marketing

**5. Cultural alignment (weight: 5-10%)**

Does the partner operate in a way compatible with the company's standards? Cultural misalignment surfaces during Deliver (quality issues) and Renew (customer satisfaction). It is hard to measure and easy to ignore. Ignoring it is expensive. [P-47]

Criteria to score:
- Customer satisfaction track record
- Ethical standards and compliance history
- Communication style and responsiveness
- Willingness to share data and collaborate transparently [P-49]
- Management stability and organizational maturity

### ABM/TAS skill: scoring matrix

The scoring matrix converts the IPP into a quantified assessment. Each criterion within each dimension receives a score from 1 to 5. Scores are multiplied by dimension weights to produce a weighted total.

**Scoring scale:**

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Does not meet minimum threshold. Disqualifying unless compensated by exceptional strength elsewhere. |
| 2 | Below expectations. Significant investment needed to bring to acceptable level. |
| 3 | Meets expectations. Adequate for a productive partnership at current capability. |
| 4 | Above expectations. Partner brings differentiated strength in this area. |
| 5 | Exceptional. Partner sets the benchmark. Rare. |

**Template (one row per partner):**

| Partner | Strategic fit (wt: __%) | Capability (wt: __%) | Market access (wt: __%) | Financial health (wt: __%) | Cultural alignment (wt: __%) | Weighted total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Partner A | [score] | [score] | [score] | [score] | [score] | [calculated] |
| Partner B | [score] | [score] | [score] | [score] | [score] | [calculated] |
| Partner N | [score] | [score] | [score] | [score] | [score] | [calculated] |

Weights are set once during program design and applied consistently across all partners. Changing weights mid-assessment to favor a specific partner defeats the purpose.

**Minimum threshold rule:** any partner scoring below 2 on any dimension requires explicit justification for continued recruitment. A partner with exceptional market access (5) but poor financial health (1) is a risk that must be acknowledged and mitigated, not ignored.

### ABM/TAS skill: GE/McKinsey 9-box prioritization

The 9-box maps scored partners into an investment grid. This is portfolio management, not individual partner evaluation. The output determines how recruitment resources, onboarding investment, and enablement budget are allocated across the partner base. [P-14]

**Axes:**

- X-axis: partner attractiveness (weighted total from the scoring matrix). High/Medium/Low.
- Y-axis: strategic fit (the strategic fit dimension score, isolated). High/Medium/Low.

**The nine cells:**

| | High attractiveness | Medium attractiveness | Low attractiveness |
|---|---|---|---|
| High strategic fit | Invest heavily. Priority recruitment. Premium onboarding. | Invest selectively. Recruit with targeted enablement plan. | Develop or watch. Strategic fit is high but current capability lags. |
| Medium strategic fit | Grow. Strong partner with room to deepen strategic alignment. | Core portfolio. Maintain and monitor. Standard onboarding. | Deprioritize. Low return on recruitment investment. |
| Low strategic fit | Harvest. Take current revenue but do not invest in growth. | Manage for efficiency. Minimal investment. | Exit or do not recruit. The partnership consumes more than it produces. [P-15] |

**Decision rules:**

- Top-left cell (high/high): recruit immediately. Assign senior relationship owner. Fast-track onboarding. These partners receive disproportionate enablement and co-sell investment because the return compounds.
- Middle cells: recruit on standard timeline. Standard onboarding. Monitor for movement toward top-left.
- Bottom-right cell (low/low): do not recruit. If already in portfolio, plan graceful exit. Every dollar spent here is a dollar not spent on a high/high partner. [P-15]

### ABM/TAS skill: Target Account List output

The scoring matrix and 9-box produce a ranked Target Account List (TAL). This is the Recruit stage's primary deliverable: a prioritized list of partners to approach, with approach strategy per tier.

**TAL structure:**

| Rank | Partner | Weighted score | 9-box cell | Recommended approach | Timeline | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | [name] | [score] | Invest heavily | Direct executive outreach. Custom value proposition. | 30 days | [name] |
| 2 | [name] | [score] | Invest selectively | Targeted outreach via referral or industry event. | 60 days | [name] |
| N | [name] | [score] | Core portfolio | Standard recruitment campaign. | 90 days | [name] |

**Approach tiers:**

- Tier 1 (invest heavily): personalized, executive-to-executive outreach. The manufacturer's leadership contacts the partner's leadership with a specific value proposition tied to the partner's strategic priorities. This is not a mass email. It is account-based selling applied to partner recruitment.
- Tier 2 (invest selectively): targeted outreach through referrals, industry events, or existing relationship networks. The value proposition is tailored but the outreach is not exclusively executive-level.
- Tier 3 (core portfolio): standard recruitment campaign. Partner portal registration, webinar invitation, inbound response. Volume approach for partners who meet minimum thresholds but are not strategic priorities.

**Refresh cadence:** the TAL is refreshed quarterly. Partners who decline or do not respond after two contact cycles are moved to a watch list. Partners who accept move into Stage 2 (Onboard). The recruitment pipeline is tracked in CRM/PRM with the same discipline as a sales pipeline: stages, conversion rates, velocity.

## Common failure modes

**Spray and pray.** Open partner program, accept all applicants, no qualification criteria. Produces a large, low-quality portfolio. The top 20% carry the bottom 80%. Channel team spends most of its time managing partners who will never produce material revenue.

**Revenue history as sole criterion.** Recruiting partners based on how much they purchased in the past, not on their fit with the future growth plan. This locks the program into yesterday's partner mix. Partners who bought large volumes of legacy product may have no interest in new product lines, new geographies, or new customer segments. [P-12]

**No exit planning.** Partners who underperform stay in the program indefinitely because no one defined the exit criteria or process. Graceful exit is part of professional partnership management. Failing to plan for it creates a portfolio that grows in headcount but not in productivity. [P-15]

**Ignoring the Build/Acquire/Partner decision.** Recruiting partners for capabilities the company should build internally. This creates dependency on external parties for core competitive functions and obscures the true cost of the capability gap.

**Single-type portfolio.** Recruiting only resellers when the business needs SIs, LSPs, and technology partners to cover the full customer journey. A one-type portfolio limits market access and creates blind spots in delivery and service.

## Diagnostic questions

1. How do you define a "good partner" today? Is that definition written down and scored, or is it in people's heads?
2. How many partners did you recruit in the last 12 months? How many of those generated revenue in the same period?
3. What percentage of your partner portfolio generates 80% of partner revenue?
4. Do you have an Ideal Partner Profile with weighted criteria? When was it last updated?
5. How do you prioritize which partners to invest in versus maintain versus exit?
6. Do you track partner recruitment as a pipeline with stages and conversion rates?
7. How do you source new partner leads? Inbound only, outbound only, or both?
8. Have you mapped which capabilities you need from partners versus building internally?
9. What is your exit process for underperforming partners? Has it been used in the last 12 months?
10. Do you use partner ecosystem data (account mapping, install base overlap) to identify recruitment targets?

## Intervention library

### Play: Ideal Partner Profile build

- **Applicability:** any company without a formalized, scored partner profile. Starting point for programs at Basic maturity.
- **Description:** workshop-based exercise (half-day) with channel leadership, sales leadership, and product leadership. Output: IPP with five dimensions, weighted criteria, and scoring scale. Validated against 10 existing partners to calibrate.
- **Source:** [P-12] [P-13] Bamford (alliance capability frameworks) + Bob Moore (ecosystem data as recruitment signal)
- **Lead time:** 2 weeks to workshop completion. 2 weeks to scoring calibration. 4 weeks total.

### Play: Build/Acquire/Partner grid

- **Applicability:** any company that has not formally decided which capabilities to source through partners. Prevents recruitment of partners for the wrong capabilities.
- **Description:** executive alignment exercise. Map every go-to-market capability against build/acquire/partner. Output: clear list of capabilities that justify partner investment. [P-10]
- **Source:** Atluri/Dietz (ecosystem strategy) + Bamford (alliance formation logic)
- **Lead time:** 1-2 executive sessions. 2-3 weeks.

### Play: scoring matrix and 9-box implementation

- **Applicability:** any company with an IPP but no systematic way to rank partners. The bridge from profile to portfolio management.
- **Description:** build the scoring matrix in a spreadsheet or PRM. Score all existing partners. Plot on 9-box. Identify the invest/maintain/exit distribution. Challenge: first scoring pass will surface uncomfortable truths about long-standing partners who score low.
- **Source:** [P-14] GE/McKinsey portfolio management adapted for partner ecosystems.
- **Lead time:** 2-3 weeks for matrix build. 1-2 weeks for initial scoring. Quarterly refresh thereafter.

### Play: ecosystem data-driven recruitment

- **Applicability:** companies at Professional maturity looking to move to World-class. Requires ecosystem data infrastructure (PRM + data mapping tool).
- **Description:** use account mapping data to identify partners whose customer base overlaps with the company's target accounts. Recruit based on data signal, not relationship signal. The Account Mapping Matrix identifies where a potential partner's customers already use complementary products. [P-05] [P-43]
- **Source:** Bob Moore (Ecosystem-Led Growth, Crossbeam model)
- **Lead time:** 60-90 days to implement data mapping. Ongoing recruitment based on signals thereafter.

### Play: partner exit protocol

- **Applicability:** any company with partners who have been in the program for 12+ months without producing revenue. Also applies to partners whose strategic fit has changed.
- **Description:** define exit criteria (minimum revenue, minimum activity, minimum certification). Communicate criteria to all partners. Execute exit with 90-day notice, transition support for end-customers, and clean contractual closure. [P-15]
- **Source:** Bamford (graceful exit as professional partnership practice)
- **Lead time:** 30 days to define criteria. 90 days for first exit cycle.

### Play: scoring model recalibration

- **Applicability:** companies at Professional maturity with an established IPP, scoring matrix, and 12+ months of recruitment data. The infrastructure exists but conversion rates are flat or declining. This is the play for programs that built the system and now need to tune it.
- **Description:** backtest the scoring model against actual outcomes. Pull every partner recruited in the last 12-18 months. Compare their IPP scores at recruitment against their actual revenue production, activation speed, and retention. Identify which scoring dimensions predicted success and which did not. Common findings: financial health and cultural alignment are under-weighted, market access is over-weighted (large customer base does not guarantee willingness to sell). Recalibrate dimension weights based on the data. Adjust minimum thresholds. Retire criteria that do not correlate with performance. Add new criteria that do (e.g., speed of response during recruitment correlates strongly with activation velocity). Scoring models degrade within 2-3 quarters if not recalibrated. Quarterly review of score distributions against outcomes is the maintenance cadence.
- **Source:** Unifyr Channel Atlas (partner scoring recalibration methodology). Pedowitz Group (closed-loop partner scoring with regression analysis).
- **Lead time:** 2-3 weeks for data pull and analysis. 1 week for recalibration. Impact visible within one recruitment cycle (one quarter).

### Play: predictive recruitment with ecosystem signals

- **Applicability:** companies at Professional maturity with ecosystem data infrastructure (PRM + data mapping tool + CRM integration) looking to move to World-class. The company already scores and ranks partners but sources candidates through manual identification or inbound applications.
- **Description:** layer predictive signals on top of the existing scoring model. Four signal categories: (1) ICP overlap: use account mapping to quantify how many of the potential partner's customers match the company's target accounts before making first contact. (2) Competitive displacement: monitor market signals for competitors losing partners, product end-of-life announcements, or M&A events that displace existing partner relationships. (3) Intent signals: track which potential partners visit the company's partner portal, download content, or attend events before any outreach occurs. (4) Success pattern matching: use historical data on which partner attributes predicted the fastest time-to-first-revenue and highest lifetime value, then score new prospects against those attributes. AI-powered partner matching platforms now automate this: PartnerStack's AI Recruit Agent, PartnerOptimizer's IPP look-alike discovery, and Crossbeam's overlap intelligence all reduce manual research by 85-90% while improving selection quality. The human remains in the loop for final qualification and relationship initiation.
- **Source:** [P-05] [P-43] [P-46] Bob Moore (ecosystem data-driven recruitment). Pedowitz Group (predictive AI for partner identification). PartnerStack (AI-powered partner matching).
- **Lead time:** 60-90 days for signal infrastructure. Ongoing recruitment based on surfaced candidates thereafter. First measurable impact on pipeline quality within one quarter.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Define Ideal Partner Profile | R, A | C | C | I (after recruitment) |
| Build/Acquire/Partner grid | C | C | R, A | N/A |
| Score and rank partners | R, A | C | I | N/A |
| 9-box portfolio prioritization | R, A | I | I | N/A |
| Tier 1 outreach (executive) | R | R (co-signer on outreach) | C (value proposition content) | Target |
| Tier 2 outreach (referral/event) | R, A | I | C | Target |
| Tier 3 outreach (campaign) | C | I | R, A (campaign execution) | Target |
| Contract negotiation | R, A | C (commercial terms) | I | R |
| Exit decision and execution | R, A | C | I | I (receives notice) |

**RACI variant: companies without dedicated channel leadership**

Not every company has a Head of Channel or a partner team when recruitment begins. Startups and mid-market B2B companies typically start with a single person acting as both program architect and partner manager, reporting to the CRO or CEO. The RACI shifts accordingly:

| Activity | Pre-channel-team owner | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Define Ideal Partner Profile | CRO or CEO (R, A), with sales leadership input (C) | The profile must reflect the company's growth plan, which lives at CRO/CEO level |
| Build/Acquire/Partner grid | CEO or product leadership (R, A) | This is a strategic capability decision, not a channel decision |
| Score and rank partners | First partner hire or CRO (R, A) | Keep the scoring discipline even with one person |
| Outreach (all tiers) | First partner hire (R), with CRO co-signing Tier 1 | Tier 1 outreach needs executive credibility. CRO involvement is the substitute for a VP of Partnerships |
| Contract negotiation | First partner hire (R), legal + CRO (A) | Commercial terms approval stays with the person who owns the P&L |
| Exit decision | CRO (A), first partner hire (R) | Exit authority must sit above the person managing the relationship |

The trigger to split roles is partner volume: when the portfolio exceeds 25-30 active partners, a single full-lifecycle manager cannot handle both recruitment and relationship management. At that point, hiring a dedicated Partner Acquisition Manager (recruitment) and a Partner Growth Manager (ongoing relationship) becomes the scaling move. Until then, the full-lifecycle model works if the person maintains the scoring and pipeline discipline described in this stage. Complexity of the team structure should follow program maturity, not precede it.
